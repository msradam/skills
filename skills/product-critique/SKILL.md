# Product critique

Sources:
- Marty Cagan, "The Four Big Risks" (SVPG) — https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/
- Paul Graham, "How to Get Startup Ideas" — https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html

## The core idea

Most product feedback is gentle because the people giving it are invested, polite, or both. That politeness is expensive: it lets a team spend a year building something nobody buys, because the uncomfortable question was never asked out loud. The job of this skill is to ask those questions, in a form specific enough to be arguable.

Cagan's framing is useful precisely because it splits "is this good?" into four risks that fail independently. A product can be loved by users and still kill the company (viability). It can be technically elegant and unusable. Treating them separately stops one strong answer from masking three weak ones.

## The four risks

Assess each separately. Do not average them into an overall verdict.

1. **Value risk** — will customers actually buy it, or users choose to use it? The hardest of the four. Evidence of people paying, switching, or repeatedly returning outranks evidence of people saying they like it.
2. **Usability risk** — can users figure out how to use it? Not "is it pretty" — can a real user reach the outcome unaided.
3. **Feasibility risk** — can the engineers build this with the time, skills, and technology actually available? Not in principle: with this team, this quarter.
4. **Business viability risk** — does it work for the rest of the business? Cagan's point is that this one is chronically under-estimated. It covers go-to-market and sales channel fit, cost structure and unit economics, legal and regulatory exposure, privacy, and whether the business can support the thing once it exists.

## Is the problem real?

Before the four risks, one pass on the premise, drawn from Graham: the strongest product ideas are ones someone actually needed, usually noticed from inside a domain, not brainstormed from outside it. Ask:

- Who specifically has this problem, and how do they solve it today? "Manually" and "in a spreadsheet" are real answers; "nobody solves it" usually means nobody has it.
- Is this a problem a small number of people have intensely, or a large number have mildly? The former is a business; the latter is usually a feature.
- Would the target user notice if this disappeared tomorrow?
- Is the team building this for a user they actually understand, or for an imagined one?

## How to apply

Read the product's own documentation, plans, and any existing feedback before critiquing. Then:

- **Be specific or stay silent.** "The positioning is unclear" is useless. "Nothing here explains why a shop owner picks this over the scheduling tool already bundled with their POS" is a finding.
- **Attack the strongest version of the plan**, not a strawman. If a risk is already addressed somewhere in the docs, say so and move on rather than scoring an easy point.
- **Separate what's assumed from what's evidenced.** Flag claims presented as settled that rest on one conversation, one demo, or the founders' own intuition.
- **Name what would change your mind.** Every serious finding should come with the cheapest test that would resolve it. A critique with no falsifier is an opinion.
- **Rank by what kills the company**, not by what's easiest to fix.
- **Do not soften the conclusion to be encouraging.** The user asked for this. Genuine strengths get stated plainly and briefly, then the critique continues; do not pad with praise to balance the tone.

When a repository has an established convention for these documents (numbered files, frontmatter, a house prose style), follow it rather than inventing a new format.
