# Marketing copy audit

Source: April Dunford, "An Introduction to Positioning" — https://aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning

## The core idea

Vague, generic-sounding marketing copy is usually a symptom of unclear positioning, not a writing problem. Positioning is the context a buyer needs to correctly understand what a product is and why it matters — without it, even well-written sentences don't land, because the reader has nothing to compare the product against or measure its value relative to. Dunford's point: you can't write sharp copy for a product whose "obviously right" frame of comparison and audience haven't actually been decided. Fix the positioning first; the copy that falls out of clear positioning is usually simple and direct, not clever.

## The five components of positioning

Before writing or judging copy, these five things should be explicit and specific — not implied, not "everyone" or "the best":

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would the buyer realistically use instead of this (including "do nothing" or a manual process)? Copy that doesn't implicitly answer this leaves the reader to guess what they're comparing against.
2. **Differentiated capabilities** — what does this have that the alternatives genuinely don't? Not a feature list — the specific things that are actually different.
3. **Value** — what does that difference actually deliver for the buyer? A capability nobody cares about isn't a selling point.
4. **Target market characteristics** — who cares most about that value? Vague audiences ("everyone," "developers") produce vague copy; a specific audience with a specific pain produces specific copy.
5. **Market category** — what frame is the product being placed in (the mental shelf a buyer files it under)? The wrong category sets the wrong expectations and the wrong comparison set, no matter how good the copy is.

## Checklist for copy itself

Once positioning is clear, check the actual copy for:

1. **What it is, fast** — can a reader who's never heard of this tell what it does within seconds, without jargon that assumes they already know the category?
2. **Specific over generic** — claims a buyer could verify or compare ("cuts build time from 10 minutes to 40 seconds") beat unfalsifiable superlatives ("blazing fast," "best-in-class").
3. **Speaks to the target market's actual language** — uses the words that specific audience uses for their problem, not internal product terminology.
4. **Value before feature list** — leads with what the buyer gets, backs it with the capability that delivers it, not the reverse.
5. **Comparison is implicit or explicit, not absent** — the copy should make sense against the competitive alternative identified above; copy that could apply to any competitor equally isn't differentiated.

## How to apply

Before writing new copy, get explicit answers to the five positioning components — from the user if you don't already have them, don't invent a target market or differentiator that hasn't been stated or verified. When auditing existing copy that feels vague or unconvincing, diagnose which positioning component is actually missing or unclear (usually target market or differentiated capability) before rewriting sentences — a rewrite without fixing the underlying positioning gap tends to just produce different-sounding vague copy.
